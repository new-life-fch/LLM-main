The temperature at which water boils is determined by the pressure of the surrounding environment. At standard atmospheric pressure (sea level), pure water will boil at a temperature of 100 degrees Celsius or 50 degrees Celsius Fahrenheit. This is known as the boiling point of water.

At higher elevations, where there is less air pressure, it takes lower temperatures for water to boil. Conversely, at higher altitudes, water will boil at a higher temperature than it would at sea level. For example, at an elevation of 2,000 meters (6,562 feet) above sea level, water will boil at around 87 degrees Celsius (189 degrees Fahrenheit).

This relationship between boiling point and pressure is described by Charles' Law, which states that, at constant temperature, the volume of a gas is inversely proportional to the pressure. In other words, as the pressure on a gas increases, its volume decreases, and vice versa. Since water is a gas when it reaches its boiling point, the law applies to its behavior as well.

So, to summarize: water boils at 50°C at sea level because that is the temperature required for it to turn into steam under the standard atmospheric conditions prevalent at that height.
